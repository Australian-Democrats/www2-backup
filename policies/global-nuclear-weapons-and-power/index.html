The plan at a glance

01 No First Use, universally

As a US ally, Australia should press for a universal commitment that nuclear weapons are never used first — the first step of the plan.

02 Fewer weapons ready to fire

A drastic reduction in the weapons kept ready for immediate use — today 3,844 are deployed with operational forces.

03 A world under 2,000 warheads

Reduce the global stockpile from roughly 13,080 to fewer than 2,000. It has fallen from over 60,000 before; it can keep falling.

04 Off hair-trigger alert

Take weapons off high alert and launch-on-warning readiness, where misinterpretation or miscalculation risks escalation to full-scale nuclear war.

05 An agenda with a pedigree

These are the recommendations of Gareth Evans, former Foreign Minister and initiator of the Canberra Commission on the Elimination of Nuclear Weapons.

The drift, and the way down

One column is where the world is heading. The other is what Australia, as a US ally, should be pressing for — abroad and at home.

Today — the drift

  • 13,080 warheads across nine states; 2,000 on high alert
  • Russia has suspended New START and revoked the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty
  • China doubling its arsenal; North Korea ran 90+ missile tests in 2022
  • Global nuclear weapons spending up $US82.9 billion; extensive modernisation programs underway
  • A $490 billion reactor plan for coal sites, with no industry, workforce or time before 2038

Our plan — the way down

  • A universal commitment to No First Use, pressed by Australia as an ally
  • Weapons taken off high alert and launch-on-warning readiness
  • Global stockpile cut to fewer than 2,000; a drastic cut in weapons ready for immediate use
  • Keep the 1999 nuclear power ban we negotiated; back the 82%-renewables grid instead

01

The Issues

So long as anyone has nuclear weapons, others will want them. So long as anyone possesses nuclear weapons, they are bound, one day, to be used, by accident or miscalculation if not by deliberate design. And any such use would be catastrophic for our world as we know it.

— Carl Rolf Ekéus, Swedish diplomat, representative to the Conference on Disarmament in Geneva

Nuclear weapons

Total elimination of nuclear weapons is surely the goal of peace-loving people around the world but the decades-long efforts of the non-nuclear states have had limited success in persuading nuclear states to disarm.

US, Russia, the UK, France, China, India, Pakistan, North Korea, and Israel possess ~13,080 nuclear weapons of mass destruction, of which 9,576 were potentially operationally available. An estimated 3,844 of these were deployed with operational forces, 2,000 of them on high operational alert. The US has 4,477 and Russia 3,708 – 90% of the total number.

US spent ~$US43.7 billion on nuclear weapons in 2022. Globally, spending on nuclear weapons increased by $US82.9 billion.

Nuclear power

The global trend suggests renewables will overtake coal as the leading power source this year. In Australia the average annual renewable share of the electricity market will be 45% in 2025. The Government’s target is 82% by 2030 which means doubling renewables in just five years.

The need for continuous baseload power is a recurring theme by some promoting nuclear power as a non-carbon emitting alternative to coal and gas. However, the obstacles are numerous:

  • Australia does not have a commercial nuclear industry nor the necessary skilled workforce. There is insufficient time to build these reactors before the outdated and now unreliable coal-fired power stations are shut down by 2038 at the latest.

  • The Opposition intends to build large reactors on coal mining and burning sites at Liddell and Mount Piper in NSW, Tarong and Callide in Qld, and Loy Yang in Vic. This would mean long delays while demolition, site clean-up (if this is even possible) were done.

  • Nuclear energy produces waste that must be stored at significant cost, deep underground for thousands of years.

  • Estimates are that these proposed new reactors would be at a cost $490 billion to taxpayers.

  • There is a legislative ban on nuclear power, negotiated by the Australian Democrats in 1999. Most state premiers and their opposition leaders are opposed to nuclear power.

  • Renewables are now much cheaper than coal and gas-fired generation and battery power will, in the not too distant future, cover the times when the wind does not blow etc. The Australian Energy Market Commission estimates that a household fully electrified will reduce its annual energy spending by 70% or around $3,500/year.

  • Australia’s competitive advantages are lots of good sun and wind, plenty of open space and a strong willingness by the population at large to reduce greenhouse emissions.

In short, building nuclear power plants in Australia is absurd – a mere political distraction. See the CSIRO’s advice.

02

Our Plan

As an ally of the US, Australia should at least be pressing for:

  • A universal commitment to No First Use

  • A drastic reduction in weapons ready for immediate use

  • Reducing the overall global stockpile to less than 2,000 weapons

  • Taking weapons off high alert and launch-on warning readiness

(Recommendations of Gareth Evans, former Foreign Minister, initiator of the Canberra Commission on the Elimination of Nuclear weapons)

03

The Evidence

We are drifting into one of the most dangerous periods in human history.

— Dan Smith, director of the Stockholm International Peace Research Institute

The regions most likely to draw the US into conflict are the Korean Peninsula, Taiwan Strait, Eastern Europe and the Persian Gulf. North Korea is developing intercontinental ballistic missiles that can reach the US.

Russia has suspended its New START Agreement, revoked the Comprehensive Test Ban Treaty and threatened to use nuclear weapons in its invasion of Ukraine.

China is in the middle of a significant modernisation and a doubling of its nuclear arsenal. Its nuclear stockpile is expected to continue growing over the coming decade and some projections suggest that it will deploy at least as many intercontinental ballistic missiles as either Russia or the USA in that period.

India and Pakistan appear to be increasing the size of their nuclear weapon inventories, and the UK has announced plans to increase its stockpile. North Korea’s military nuclear program remains central to its national security strategy and it may have assembled up to 30 nuclear weapons and could produce more. North Korea conducted more than 90 ballistic missile tests during 2022—the highest number it has ever undertaken in a single year. Israel continues to maintain its long-standing policy of nuclear ambiguity, leaving significant uncertainty about the number and characteristics of its nuclear weapons.

The UN says the risk of nuclear weapons use is higher now than at any time since the cold war.

Nuclear weapons

Nuclear weapons numbers have come down from highs of over 60,000, primarily due to the US and Russia dismantling retired warheads. However both have extensive and expensive programs underway to replace and modernise their nuclear warheads, and their missile, aircraft and submarine delivery systems. Today’s nuclear weapons are more advanced in design, technology, and destructive power. They are more precise and ten or more times more powerful than the early atomic bombs.

They are also smaller, allowing integration into various delivery systems. Many have multiple warheads and some are designed for close targets like battlefields. This means they are at high risk of escalation from potential misinterpretation, miscalculation, or an unintended response from adversaries, all of which could lead to full-scale nuclear war.
